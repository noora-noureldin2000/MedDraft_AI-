# Academic Language and Style

Academic language is the formal register of scholarly communication. It is formal (no slang, contractions, or casual tone), objective (facts and evidence, not personal emotion), precise (specific terminology, not vague expressions), cohesive (logical flow with clear transitions), and evidence-based (claims supported by citations, no unsupported generalizations).

## Formality

Use full forms ("do not", not "don't"). Avoid colloquial language ("children", not "kids"). Keep a steady level of formality throughout; do not jump between formal and casual styles.

Bad: "Kids these days don't study enough."
Good: "Recent studies suggest that contemporary students dedicate fewer hours to academic preparation compared to past cohorts (Smith, 2021)."

Bad: "I think the results are good."
Good: "The results indicate a statistically significant improvement in performance."

## Objectivity

Focus on the research, not personal emotions. Use third-person perspective where the discipline expects it; first-person plural ("we") is conventional and preferred where active voice is used for study actions and interpretations.

## Maintaining consistency

Use language consistently from start to finish: repeat technical terms and acronyms verbatim; keep tense straight (usually past tense for methods and results, present tense for established facts and conclusions, modal verbs for speculation and recommendations); keep one citation format throughout. Consistency keeps readers engaged with the message instead of distracted by word-level variation.

## Transitions and their function

Transitions should state explicitly how new material connects to earlier material: restate or point back to prior ideas, mark conceptual turns, and show how the argument builds logically. Do not overuse bare additives such as "further" or "moreover"; choose connectives that name the relationship (contrast, cause, elaboration, example).

## Eliminating ambiguity

Every sentence should specifically support the argument. Give every pronoun ("it", "this", "they") a clear antecedent. Name relationships precisely ("associated with", "caused by", "adjusted for") instead of bare "related". Never imply causal connections the study did not establish. Replace vague words ("a lot", "nice", "good") with precise terms ("numerous", "significant", "statistically significant improvement").

## Enhancing readability

Make the paper reader-friendly without compromising content: one main idea per paragraph, usually opened with a topic sentence, with paragraph breaks marking idea boundaries. Vary sentence structure for interest but always prioritize legibility over styling. Prefer concrete examples, numbers, and citations over abstract claims.
