# Medical Discussion Writer

A specialized skill for authoring, structuring, and revising the Discussion section of medical, nursing, public health, and clinical theses or journal manuscripts.

## When to Use

- Writing the Discussion section of a clinical thesis, dissertation, or manuscript.
- Transforming raw statistical tables and results into an interpretative scientific narrative.
- Benchmarking study findings against external literature (identifying agreements and disagreements).
- Incorporating clinical and methodological rationales ("Researcher's Perspective").
- Reviewing or critiquing existing draft discussions to eliminate redundant data dumps and raw table commentary.

## Core Rules & Anti-Patterns

- Never copy-paste raw results or table comments: The Discussion is an interpretation, not a rerun of the Results chapter. Do not report comprehensive frequency tables, standard deviations, exact counts, or repetitive p-values.
- Convert statistics to qualitative narrative terms: Use proportional phrasing such as "the majority," "more than two-thirds," "less than one-third," "a minor proportion," or "a statistically significant positive correlation."
- Always pair agreements with disagreements: If findings align with regional or national literature, actively search for and contrast against divergent international or conflicting studies.
- Mandatory clinical justification: Never leave an agreement or discrepancy without explaining why (demographics, sample size, protocol variations, local healthcare constraints, cultural factors, or training gaps).

## Standard Structural Framework

### 1. Introductory Framing (1-2 Paragraphs)

- Global Context & Burden: Open with authoritative global health context (e.g., WHO guidelines, disease burden, clinical stakes).
- Core Clinical Rationale: Highlight the critical role of the clinical intervention, healthcare provider, or diagnostic target.
- Study Aim: Reiterate the central objective/aim of the study clearly and concisely.

### 2. Variable-by-Variable Core Body (The 4-Step Micro-Formula)

Every thematic section or clinical variable must follow this 4-step sequence:

- Step 1: State the Finding (Narrative Summary):
  - Starter: "Regarding [variable]...", "The findings of the present study revealed that..."
  - Rule: Summarize the qualitative trend (e.g., "more than half of the studied nurses demonstrated unsatisfactory practice...").
- Step 2: Benchmark of Agreement:
  - Starter: "This finding aligns with / is consistent with / is supported by [Author et al. (Year)], who also reported that..."
- Step 3: Benchmark of Disagreement / Contrast:
  - Starter: "However, this finding contradicts / contrasts with / is not supported by [Author et al. (Year)], who observed that..."
- Step 4: Researcher's Perspective (Scientific & Clinical Justification):
  - Starter: "From the researcher's perspective / The researcher suggests that these findings may be attributed to..."
  - Content: Supply 2-4 concrete, contextual reasons (workload, resource availability, protocol adherence, training deficits, diagnostic criteria).

### 3. Inferential Statistics & Correlations

Address bivariate and multivariate associations (e.g., knowledge vs. practice, experience vs. performance). Explain positive, negative, or non-significant relationships with theoretical frameworks (e.g., why years of experience might plateau without continuous medical education).

### 4. Overall Synthesis & Perspective

Conclude the section with an overarching clinical takeaway, institutional recommendations, or systemic implications for practice and policy.

## Standard Phrasing & Transitions

- Opening Variables: "Regarding [X]...", "Concerning [X]...", "In relation to [X]...", "As regards [X]..."
- Reporting Agreement: "These findings align with...", "This finding is consistent with the studies by...", "This result agrees with...", "This is supported by..."
- Reporting Contrast: "However, this finding contradicts...", "While, this result contradicted...", "However, these findings contrast with...", "This finding is not supported by..."
- Attributing Justifications: "From the researcher's perspective, this may be attributed to...", "The researcher suggests that...", "According to the researcher, these results could be due to..."
