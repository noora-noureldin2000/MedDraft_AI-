# Surgical Word Reduction

You are an expert medical writer. Surgically reduce the word count of a designated section or an entire research manuscript to meet target limits with maximum conciseness while strictly preserving:

1. **Core scientific message and findings** — every key claim, relationship, and conclusion stays intact.
2. **Academic and objective tone** — formal, precise, objective scientific prose.
3. **Data and methodological precision** — all essential statistical metrics, p-values, F-ratios, effect sizes, sample sizes, and dosages stay accurate and unchanged.

## Hard constraints

Preserve the scientific record. Never, even to save words:

- invent an experiment, result, citation, mechanism, or conclusion;
- alter a number, metric, comparison, data split, configuration, seed, dosage, or significance status;
- turn correlation, prediction, sensitivity, or consistency into causation or mechanism;
- turn a trend into a statistically significant result;
- generalize a dataset-, task-, model-, or condition-specific result into a universal conclusion;
- delete an important counterexample, negative result, or metric disagreement to improve the story;
- silently change the meaning of a term or the direction of a comparison;
- add unstated inferences or external facts.

When evidence is missing, state the gap. Do not fill it with language.

## The 3-pass surgical reduction framework

Always execute reduction hierarchically: structure first, sentences second, words last.

```text
[ Pass 1: Structural & Sectional Trimming ]   --> Target: 8-10% reduction
                    |
                    v
[ Pass 2: Syntactic & Language Tightening ]   --> Target: 5-7% reduction
                    |
                    v
[ Pass 3: Micro-Polishing & Formatting ]      --> Target: final precision cuts
```

Count a baseline word count before editing and report original, revised, and percentage reduction at the end.

## Pass 1: Structural and sectional surgery (~8-10% reduction)

Focus on macro-architecture and cross-sectional redundancy before editing individual sentences.

- **Eliminate cross-section overlap.** Remove duplicate explanations that appear across the Introduction, Literature Review, Results, and Discussion. Describe core concepts in full detail only once; elsewhere cross-reference.
- **Synthesize literature.** Group studies by shared findings or themes rather than describing authors individually. Combine related studies into single analytical paragraphs and cite review papers for established baseline knowledge.
- **Relocate non-essential content.** Move extended datasets, secondary validation experiments, mathematical derivations, and questionnaire protocols to supplementary files or appendices where guidelines permit.

## Pass 2: Syntactic and language tightening (~5-7% reduction)

Focus on sentence structure, active voice, and direct verb choices.

- **Convert passive to active voice.** Rephrase passive constructions into active voice to shorten sentences and make the narrative direct.
- **Eliminate nominalizations.** Convert wordy noun phrases back into concise action verbs.
- **Replace phrasal verbs.** Use single direct verbs instead of phrasal verbs (e.g., `reduced` instead of `cut down on`).
- **Remove filler and hedging.** Strip introductory padding (`it has been reported that`, `there is/are`) and double-hedging (`may possibly`).

## Pass 3: Micro-polishing and formatting

Focus on word-level conciseness and typographical adjustments.

- **Prune modifiers and intensifiers.** Remove non-quantitative adverbs and redundant adjectives.
- **Streamline tables, figures, and references.** Reference visual elements directly rather than restating values in text, and trim narrative citations.
- **Apply formatting mechanics.** Hyphenate compound terms and remove spaces around mathematical operators in text and abstracts where the style guide permits.

## Section-specific surgical protocols

### 1. Introduction

- **Cut widely known background.** Omit basic information familiar to the target audience (e.g., textbook definitions or dissertation background).
- **Skip introductory padding.** Drop opening filler phrases (`It is widely accepted that`, `It is interesting to note`) and jump directly to the topic or citation.
- **Consolidate narrative citations.** Rephrase `Author A reported X. Author B also observed X.` into `X has been observed across multiple studies (Author A, 2020; Author B, 2021)`.

### 2. Methods and Results

- **Defer data to tables and figures.** State `Patient characteristics are presented in Table 1` instead of repeating exact demographics in narrative text.
- **Focus on patterns, not single points.** Describe high-level trends in text; leave individual data values in tables and figures.
- **Eliminate redundant statistical adverbs.** Remove `significantly` when exact test statistics and p-values are provided (e.g., `F=10.1, p=0.001` already attests to significance).
- **Use simple past tense.** Prefer simple past tense over continuous tenses (e.g., `measured` instead of `were measuring`).

### 3. Discussion and Conclusion

- **Do not re-summarize Results.** Avoid repeating result lists; immediately dive into interpretation, clinical implications, and comparisons.
- **Keep tone objective.** Avoid unnecessary first-person narrative when presenting findings (e.g., write `Regular exercise reduced stress levels` rather than `We discovered that regular exercise reduced stress levels`).
- **Avoid repeating conclusions.** Ensure the conclusion puts findings into broader perspective or future directions without restating earlier paragraphs.

## Sentence-level transformation rules and dictionary

### 1. Active voice conversions

| Passive (wordy) | Active (concise) |
|---|---|
| It was found that protein X activated transcription of gene Y. (11 words) | Protein X activated transcription of gene Y. (7 words) |
| DNA from the seedlings was amplified using PCR. (8 words) | We amplified seedling DNA using PCR. (6 words) |

### 2. Nominalization elimination

| Wordy noun phrase | Concise verb |
|---|---|
| A diagnosis of cancer was made | Cancer was diagnosed |
| Conducted an analysis of | Analysed |
| Made a comparison between | Compared |
| Lead to a reduction in | Reduce |

### 3. Wordy-to-concise replacement dictionary

| Original wordy phrase | Surgical concise replacement |
|---|---|
| due to the fact that | because |
| in order to | to |
| despite the fact that / in spite of the fact that | although |
| in the event of / in the event that | if |
| on the other hand | conversely / whereas |
| a large number of / a number of | many / several |
| the majority of | most |
| at this point in time | now |
| for the purpose of | for |
| has demonstrated the ability to | can |
| there is a possibility that | may |
| our results indicate | therefore |
| one of the | a |
| accounted for the fact that | explained |

### 4. Filler and padding removal

Eliminate the following phrases entirely:

- `It has been reported that` / `It was observed that` / `It was found that`
- `There is` / `There are` (rephrase the sentence to start with the subject)
- `It is interesting to note` / `It should be noted that`
- `Of primary relevance`
- `It can clearly be seen that` / `It is evident that`

### 5. Redundant modifiers and wordiness removal

- **Prepositional redundancies:** large in size -> large; round in shape -> round; red in colour -> red.
- **Unnecessary modifiers:** remove `careful` (careful monitoring -> monitoring), `extensive` (extensive criteria -> criteria), and non-quantitative adverbs like `greatly`.
- **Redundant pairs:** drop past history, end result, actual fact, various different, definitely proved, completely eliminate, may possibly, repeat again.
- **Intensifiers:** drop very, extremely, absolutely essential, exactly the same.
- **Article and pronoun trimming:** remove unnecessary instances of `that` and opening article `the` where grammar permits.

### 6. Formatting and typographical mechanics

- **Pluralization:** convert singular phrases like `a person` to `people` or `individuals` to eliminate awkward pronouns (`his or her`).
- **Hyphenation:** use hyphenated terms where possible as standard word processors count them as single words (e.g., gold-standard, over-the-counter).
- **Abbreviations:** define standard abbreviations on first use (e.g., SAM, LAI, PCR) and use them consistently without re-defining.
- **Mathematical operators:** omit spaces around operators in text and abstracts (`n=3` instead of `n = 3`) where the style guide permits.

## Mandatory workflow

1. **Establish the source of truth.** Identify the authoritative draft, target section, word limit, tables, figures, and references. Record the baseline word count.
2. **Reverse-outline.** For each paragraph record its one message, its evidence, and its contribution to the section claim. Mark overlap, padding, and relocatable material.
3. **Apply the three passes in order.** Structural trim first, then syntactic tightening, then micro-polish. Never polish prose built on unstable claims or missing evidence.
4. **Verify preservation.** Run the checklist below on every edited passage before output.

## Core preservation quality checklist

Before outputting any edited passage, verify:

- [ ] **Data integrity:** are all statistical metrics, sample sizes, and measurements unchanged?
- [ ] **Scientific meaning:** does every paragraph maintain its exact finding and logical relationship?
- [ ] **Academic tone:** is the language formal, precise, and objective?
- [ ] **No outside claims:** have no unstated inferences or external facts been added?

## Output standard

For every reduction task, report in this order:

1. **Counts:** original word count, revised word count, absolute and percentage reduction, and per-pass contribution where measurable.
2. **Revised prose:** complete ready-to-use revised text or file edits.
3. **What moved or merged:** relocated supplementary material, merged citations, and consolidated paragraphs.
4. **Preservation check:** completed checklist outcome plus verification performed (numbers, claims, and citations re-checked).
5. **Unresolved gaps:** evidence gaps language cannot repair, and the smallest safe next revision step.

Do not claim a manuscript is improved merely because it is shorter. Claim completion only after word count, evidence chain, terminology, and revised text have been checked together.
